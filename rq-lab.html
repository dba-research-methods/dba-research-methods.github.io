01 · The case

Walmart's supply chain, read as evidence

A success story is a record of decisions. Each decision is evidence of a problem that was solved in practice — and often one that is unresolved in theory. That second layer is where research lives.

Just-in-time inventory

Decisions as evidence

Restocking tuned to demand to avoid overstocking and understocking — solving a forecasting and coordination problem at scale.

Transportation optimisation

Cost reduction

Analysis of shipping data and logistics workflows to cut costs — a routing and network design problem with mature, though partial, answers.

Supplier resilience

Adaptive sourcing

Supplier analysis built chains able to absorb disasters and pandemics — an open question about what, precisely, makes a chain resilient.

Data-driven advantage

Competitive edge

Continuous research investment as a moat — raising the question of how firms generalise lessons beyond one retailer's context.

Known — solved in practice

  • JIT restocking reduces overstock and understock at store level.
  • Transportation analytics cut shipping costs.
  • Supplier analysis improved resilience during disasters and pandemics.

Unknown — unresolved in theory

  • How much buffer is optimal under variable disruption risk?
  • Which supplier attributes best predict resilience, and how are they measured?
  • How do forecasts hold when consumer behaviour departs from history (panic buying)?
The reframe. The success story answers "what did Walmart do?" Research asks "what general law or boundary condition does this reveal?" The gap between those two questions is your research problem.

03 · Scoping

Investigate the area of inquiry

Almost nobody starts with a problem. You start with an area — "supply chain resilience", "AI in customer service", "hybrid work". An area is not researchable: it has no gap, no unit of analysis and no stated consequence. Investigating the area is the work that turns a topic into something the framework in the next section can bite on.

Move 01Decompose

Break the inquiry into components

An area is a bundle. Name its actors, its mechanism, its outcome and its setting separately, and each becomes a candidate boundary for the study. An undecomposed area produces the questions supervisors send back: too broad to answer, too vague to falsify.

Move 02Map

Map existing research to find the contested edge

This is not yet the literature review that verifies a gap — that comes later, in Section 08. Here you read to find where findings stop agreeing, so you know which component is worth a gap claim at all. Scoping reading is wide and shallow; verification reading is narrow and deep.

Four lenses that decompose any area

Run these four questions at the area and it stops being a topic. Each one isolates a different component, and each points at a different gap type from Section 05.

L1Boundary

Where does it succeed, and where does it fail?

The performance edge is where the variance lives, and variance is the only thing a study can explain. Uniform success is a press release; the interesting cases are the conditions that flip the outcome.

L2Mechanism

How does it actually work?

Open the box. Distinguishing two mechanisms the literature lumps together is frequently the whole contribution — a rule-based system and a learned one share a label and share almost nothing else.

L3Perception

How is it experienced, against what it replaced?

Name the comparator and the metrics before you collect anything: speed, resolution quality, trust, effort. "Better" with no comparator is the most common unfalsifiable claim in DBA proposals.

L4Deployment

How is it used in the wild, by whom, and at which step?

Designed use and actual use diverge, and the space between them is a standing source of the practical-knowledge gap — the one an applied doctorate is built to occupy.

The workbook runs one case throughout so the three problems stay comparable — but the lenses are not Walmart-specific, and it is worth seeing them land somewhere else before you point them at your own area.

  1. BoundaryIn which query classes do AI chatbots resolve the request, and in which do they generate frustration or escalation?
  2. MechanismIs the system rule-based, retrieval-based, or a generative language model — and does the answer change which queries it can hold?
  3. PerceptionHow do customers rate the exchange against a human agent on speed, resolution quality and empathy — and do those three move together?
  4. DeploymentWhere do platforms actually place them — pre-sales enquiry, post-sales support, returns — and is the placement chosen by evidence or by cost?

Note the shape of the fourth: it asks how a practice is chosen, not whether the technology works. That is a practical-knowledge gap, and it is the kind an executive scholar can answer better than a full-time academic can.

The failure this prevents. A problem statement written straight from an area produces a current state everyone already accepts and a gap nobody can dispute — which means nobody can be wrong, which means there is nothing to find out. Scope first, then write. The framework in Section 04 assumes you already know which component you are making a claim about.

04 · The framework

Current state → gap → consequences

An evidence-based problem statement is a three-part argument. Ellis and Levy's framework of problem-based research insists the problem be studied, not just solved — the engine of that argument is the gap.

AContext

Establish the current state

Anchor the reader in what is known and done — cite the case and the scholarly record.

BTurn

Name the gap precisely

A gap must be specific enough to study: not "we don't know much", but "no empirical test of X under Y".

CStakes

State the consequences

Show who is harmed and how much. The consequences justify the study's existence.

DCommitment

Issue the research question

Close with a question that is empirical, answerable, and bounded — the contract of the whole study.

Problem statement template

Many [organisations] currently [operate in a way described by evidence]. However, [the specific knowledge that is missing or contested] is not well understood. As a result, [who suffers, and what they lose], eroding [stakes]. This study will examine [bounded phenomenon] using [unit of analysis].

The seven research gaps

Miles' taxonomy of research gaps gives you vocabulary to say exactly what kind of gap you're addressing — which makes the problem statement auditable.

01Evidence

Evidence gap

Research findings are contradictory or sparse, so no conclusion can be drawn. "Studies disagree on whether JIT increases or decreases disruption risk."

02Knowledge

Knowledge gap

Desired knowledge does not exist, or exists only in theory and not in practice. "No measurement of how much buffer Walmart's resilience actually requires."

03Practical-knowledge

Practical-knowledge gap

Professional behaviour or decisions deviate from ideal practices. "Firms under-respond to disruption warnings despite known vulnerability."

04Methodological

Methodological gap

A better method or instrument is needed to study the phenomenon. "No validated instrument measures supply chain resilience attributes."

05Empirical

Empirical gap

A claim needs to be evaluated empirically or tested in a new setting. "Forecast-failure models exist for normal demand but are untested on panic demand."

06Theoretical

Theoretical gap

Theory is inadequate to explain a phenomenon. "Resilience theory lacks a mechanism linking inventory policy to disruption absorption."

07Population

Population gap

The population studied differs from the population of interest. "Resilience findings come from manufacturing; retail remains under-studied."

Why seven and not five

A five-gap version of this taxonomy circulates widely — evidence, methodological, empirical, theoretical, knowledge, arranged on a star. Those are cards 01, 02, 04, 05 and 06 above. It invents nothing Miles did not already put there, so the only question worth asking of it is what it drops.

It drops practical-knowledge and population — the two a DBA most often occupies. An applied doctorate is largely the study of practitioner behaviour deviating from established best practice, and of findings imported from one sector into another without being retested there. Claim a theoretical gap while sitting in a population gap and a reviewer will find it before you defend.

It also blurs two of the five it keeps, defining an evidence gap as "a lack of empirical data or research evidence" and an empirical gap as "a lack of studies or data" — the same sentence twice. Miles separates them, and so do cards 01 and 05: an evidence gap is findings that contradict each other; an empirical gap is a claim nobody has tested.

06 · The harvest

Three researchable problems

Each problem is written in full statement form, annotated with its gap type and the research question it commits to.

Problem 01

Empirical gap

Demand forecasting under extreme disruption

STATE

Retailers forecast demand from historical purchasing patterns, and Walmart's JIT system restocks on that basis.

GAP

Existing forecast models assume demand stability and have not been validated against panic-buying behaviour during pandemics and disasters.

RESULT

Stockouts at critical times, empty shelves, lost sales, and eroding customer trust across the retail industry.

Research question

How do retail demand-forecast models perform under exogenous demand shocks such as pandemics and natural disasters, and which adjustments preserve service levels?

Why it's researchable

  • Multiple organisations: all demand-driven retailers face panic demand.
  • Knowledge gap: shock-demand modelling is under-tested (Ivanov, 2020).
  • Theory + practice: informs forecasting theory and crisis planning.

Problem 02

Theoretical gap

The efficiency–resilience trade-off in lean inventory

STATE

Lean just-in-time systems minimise inventory and free capital, and are the benchmark Walmart helped make mainstream.

GAP

The optimal balance between lean efficiency and resilience buffers under variable disruption risk has no unified theoretical account — lean scholarship is conflicted (Holweg, 2007).

RESULT

Firms oscillate between costly overstocking and crisis-driven stockouts — wasted capital or lost customers.

Research question

How should buffer inventory scale with the probability, severity, and cost of disruption, and what resilience thresholds justify sacrificing lean efficiency?

Why it's researchable

  • Multiple organisations: every lean chain (auto, pharma, electronics).
  • Knowledge gap: no settled theory of the trade-off.
  • Theory + practice: extends operations theory; directly guides policy.

Problem 03

Methodological gap

Supplier attributes that predict resilience

STATE

Walmart analyses supplier relationships to build resilient chains, and supplier diversity is a recognised goal.

GAP

Which supplier attributes — geographic spread, contract terms, multi-sourcing depth — most strongly predict resilience has not been operationalised or measured.

RESULT

Single-source dependencies create cascading failures; disruptions propagate unpredictably through supplier networks.

Research question

What measurable supplier-portfolio attributes distinguish resilient from fragile supply chains, and how can they be validly and reliably assessed?

Why it's researchable

  • Multiple organisations: cross-industry supply chain risk.
  • Knowledge gap: no validated measurement instrument exists.
  • Theory + practice: tool-building with direct managerial utility.
Worked statement — Problem 02

Many retailers currently run lean just-in-time inventory systems that minimise holding costs and free up capital, yet leave supply chains exposed when demand or supply is disrupted. However, the optimal balance between efficiency and resilience — how much buffer inventory a firm should hold given the probability and cost of disruption — is not well understood in the operations literature. As a result, firms oscillate between costly overstocking and crisis-driven stockouts, eroding both margin and customer trust. This study will examine the efficiency–resilience trade-off in inventory policy under disruption risk, using Walmart's supply chain as an embedded case.

07 · The audit

Three criteria for a researchable problem

Pressure-test every candidate problem against all three criteria. A problem that fails any one is a story, not a study.

Researchability rubric

pass / fail per problem
Is this problem faced by multiple organisations?

Generalises beyond Walmart. Would a competitor face the same question? Is the phenomenon industry-level, not firm-idiosyncratic?

×3
Is there a knowledge gap research could address?

If the literature is silent or contradictory, a gap exists. Distinguish "not yet known" from "not knowable".

×3
Would solving this benefit theory and practice?

Refines or challenges an existing theory, and informs managerial or policy decisions. Name both audiences.

×3

9 = ready
9

All three criteria clearly met; question is bounded and answerable.

6

Generalises and has a gap, but the unit of analysis or constructs are loose.

Kill test: If the answer already exists in a textbook or an algorithm, it's engineering. Research starts where the textbook ends.

08 · Research practice

Academic moves that make problems credible

The craft of problem-mining draws on established methodology scholarship. These are the moves that separate a persuasive problem statement from an anecdote.

01Problematise

Problematise, don't just gap-spot

Alvesson and Sandberg challenge the habit of locating a hole in the literature. Stronger studies challenge an assumption the literature takes for granted — here, that lean efficiency and resilience are compatible by default.

02Review

Build the literature review first

Boote and Beile show the review is the foundation of the whole dissertation. Before writing a gap, verify it: coverage, synthesis, methodology critique, significance, and rhetorical clarity.

03Case

Reason with case-study logic

Yin: a single case like Walmart supports analytic generalisation to theory, not statistical generalisation to populations. Name your unit of analysis — firm, product category, or supply network.

04Bound

Define constructs operationally

Creswell: "resilience", "lean inventory", and "disruption" must be measurable before they can be studied. An undefined construct makes the gap untestable.

05Feasible

Test feasibility and access

Leedy and Ormrod: the finest question is worthless without data access, time, and ethics clearance. Design the study to the evidence you can actually obtain.

06Grounded

Ground in discipline scholarship

Anchor in the resilience canon — Christopher and Peck, Sheffi, and Ivanov on epidemic disruption, and Holweg's critique of lean — so the gap is argued against what is actually known.

How this workbook is built Evidence rules, gap taxonomy, and honesty notes Inspect method

Four editorial rules applied to every claim:

  1. 1Facts, then gaps. The case evidence appears only as current state; every gap is derived from an absence or contradiction in the scholarly record.
  2. 2One framework throughout. Every problem is forced through current state → gap → consequences so the three are comparable.
  3. 3Named gap types. Miles' taxonomy labels each problem's gap, making the audit transparent rather than rhetorical.
  4. 4Sources beat opinions. Methodology guidance cites the originating scholarship; the reference list allows verification.

Found an error? This workbook is meant to be corrected, remixed, and argued with. Cite-check anything before you use it in an assignment.

09 · The design catalogue

Research designs, with worked examples

Eighteen designs across three paradigms. A design is a plan for answering a specific kind of question — choose by the question your gap implies, never by fashion. Each card pairs the design with an example grounded in the Walmart supply chain case.

Quantitative designs 10

Measure, count, and test relationships in numbers. Best when constructs can be operationalised and data are accessible.

01Quant

Exploratory Research

Probes an ill-defined problem when little is known; builds hypotheses and orientation rather than conclusions.

Example — Interview logistics managers about how Walmart's stores actually coped with panic demand in 2020, before any hypotheses exist.

02Quant

Descriptive Research

Precisely describes the characteristics of a population, event, or process — the "what is", without explaining why.

Example — Profile the supplier disruptions Walmart faced across 2015–2025: categories, frequencies, durations, and geography.

03Quant

Correlational Research

Measures the direction and strength of association between variables, without manipulation. Correlation is not causation.

Example — Test whether supplier count correlates with stockout rate across 500 stores, reporting r and its confidence bounds.

04Quant

Causal-Comparative (Ex Post Facto)

Compares groups on an outcome after a cause has already occurred and cannot be manipulated.

Example — Compare stockout rates between stores that adopted JIT and stores that kept safety stock, after implementation.

05Quant

Experimental Research

Establishes cause–effect by manipulating an independent variable under controlled conditions, with random assignment.

Example — Randomly assign store clusters to two forecasting algorithms and compare forecast accuracy.

06Quant

Quasi-Experimental Research

Cause–effect testing when random assignment is impossible; relies on naturally occurring groups or staggered rollout.

Example — Compare regions that adopted a resilience program vs. regions that did not, controlling for known confounds.

07Quant

Survey Research

Standardised data collection from a sample to describe or explain attitudes, behaviours, or organisational traits.

Example — Survey 300 retail supply chain managers on the resilience practices they use and how effective they rate them.

08Quant

Cross-Sectional Research

One snapshot of many units at a single point in time — fast and broad, but captures no change over time.

Example — Measure resilience attributes across 200 firms at one moment to rank practices by prevalence.

09Quant

Longitudinal Research

Tracks the same units across time to observe change, sequencing, and trends.

Example — Follow monthly inventory and stockout data across Walmart stores for a decade to trace efficiency–resilience shifts.

10Quant

Time-Series / Panel (Econometric)

Models relationships from repeated observations over time to detect patterns, trends, and shocks.

Example — Estimate how shipping costs respond to oil-price shocks using a decade of firm-level panel data.

Qualitative designs 6

Build deep understanding from words, actions, and meanings — ideal when constructs are not yet measurable or context matters.

11Qual

Case Study Research

In-depth, multi-source study of a bounded case in its real-life context, enabling analytic generalisation to theory.

Example — A single embedded case of Walmart's supply chain transformation, triangulating documents, interviews, and data.

12Qual

Ethnography

Understands a group's culture and practices from within, through extended field immersion.

Example — Embed in a distribution centre to observe how workers enact JIT protocols — and where the rules break down.

13Qual

Grounded Theory

Builds a theory inductively from data rather than testing a pre-existing framework.

Example — Generate a theory of resilience from open interviews with supply chain managers, coding upward to categories.

14Qual

Phenomenology

Describes the lived experience of a phenomenon for a group of people.

Example — Capture how warehouse managers experienced the 2020 toilet-paper shortage and what being "out of stock" meant to them.

15Qual

Narrative Research

Collects and interprets people's stories to reveal how they make meaning of events.

Example — Elicit executives' career narratives around crisis supply chain decisions and the meanings they attach.

16Qual

Action Research

Solves a real problem in context through cycles of plan–act–observe–reflect, generating both change and knowledge.

Example — Partner with a retailer to pilot a forecast change, measure outcomes, reflect, and iterate.

Mixed & applied designs 2

Combine paradigms, or design artifacts — where the DBA tradition most often makes its contribution.

17Mixed

Mixed Methods Research

Integrates quantitative and qualitative strands for completeness, explanation, or triangulation.

Example — Pair a quantitative stockout dataset with supplier interviews to explain why the numbers move as they do.

18Applied

Design Science Research

Builds and evaluates an artifact — a model, method, or system — that solves a practical problem and extends knowledge.

Example — Design and field-test a resilience-score dashboard for supplier portfolios, then evaluate its usefulness.

Choosing a design by your question

If the problem statement commits to a research question, the design should select itself. Match your question stem to the design.

Your research question sounds like… Design to reach for
"What is going on here? Where do we even start?"Exploratory research
"What are the characteristics of the phenomenon?"Descriptive research
"How strongly do variables move together?"Correlational research
"Does X cause Y, and can I control the conditions?"Experimental / Quasi-experimental
"How do the groups differ after the fact?"Causal-comparative
"How does the phenomenon behave across time?"Longitudinal / Time-series
"How do people experience or make sense of it?"Phenomenology / Narrative / Ethnography
"Can I build a theory straight from the data?"Grounded theory
"Can I fix a real problem while learning?"Action / Design science
"I have both numbers and stories — how do they fit?"Mixed methods
Design is downstream of the question. A problem statement that ends with a precise question has already picked its design. If you find yourself choosing a design first, you are likely working backwards from a favourite method.

10 · The inquiry spectrum

Structured or unstructured inquiry?

Before picking a design, decide how open or fixed the inquiry should be. Structure is a choice about how much you already know — not a measure of quality. Every study sits somewhere on the spectrum, and the poles map onto two logics: deduction and induction.

Structured Deductive

Structured research

Definition — Inquiry conducted under a pre-specified, fixed plan: the research question, hypotheses, instruments, sampling, and analysis procedures are decided before data collection and deliberately not changed as data arrive.

Design hallmarks

  • Closed-ended instruments — questionnaires, structured interviews, observation checklists.
  • Probability sampling and standardised procedures for replicability.
  • Statistical analysis; tests hypotheses; aims at generalisation.
  • Rigour judged by reliability, validity, and objectivity.
Example — Test the hypothesis "JIT adoption reduces stockout frequency" by surveying 300 retail supply chain managers with fixed Likert scales, then running regression analysis.
Unstructured Inductive

Unstructured research

Definition — Inquiry conducted with an open, evolving design: questions, procedures, and even the focus of study can shift as understanding develops, and no hypotheses are fixed in advance.

Design hallmarks

  • Open-ended interviews, participant observation, and field notes.
  • Purposive sampling and emergent, adaptive procedures.
  • Thematic analysis; builds concepts and theory from the data.
  • Rigour judged by credibility, confirmability, and trustworthiness.
Example — Conduct open-ended interviews with warehouse managers to discover how JIT protocols are actually enacted, coding their own words into categories — no hypothesis stated in advance.
Middle ground Abductive

Semi-structured inquiry

Definition — The most common stance in DBA work: a pre-planned guide (themes, questions, instruments) combined with open follow-up that lets participants redirect the flow. The logic moves back and forth between data and theory.

Example — Interview suppliers using a fixed protocol of twelve questions about resilience, but chase new threads as they surface — the guide is a contract, the follow-up is discovery.

Structure is a stance, not a ranking. Structured is not "better" than unstructured — it is appropriate when constructs are already measurable and inappropriate when they are not. Fix the pole from the state of knowledge first; then the design (Section 09) selects itself.

11 · DBA practice

The applied lens — and the AI guardrails that keep it honest

A DBA is practitioner scholarship: pragmatic, practical, and applied. The standard is not "advance theory for its own sake" but solve a real problem and defend the solution rigorously. As a DBA candidate — and a student of AI and Generative AI — you are doubly positioned: GenAI can accelerate every stage of the workflow, and only disciplined guardrails keep that acceleration honest.

A spectrum, not a wall: many theses sit between the poles. The DBA simply tilts the balance toward practice — the framework in Section 04 is built for exactly that tilt.

Generative AI in the research workflow

Studying AI and GenAI as a DBA candidate puts you ahead of the guardrail curve: you can critique the instrument while using it. Same tool, two outcomes — the difference is discipline.

Where it can help

  • Literature discovery and summarisation — always back to the original source.
  • Drafting and restructuring — outlines, plain-language rewrites, clarity edits.
  • Interview transcription and cleaning (with participant consent).
  • Analysis assistance — coding scaffolds, data-visualisation ideas, query drafting.

Where it can harm

  • Hallucinated references — plausible citations that do not exist.
  • Fabricated or "smoothed" data that no longer reflect what was collected.
  • Undisclosed copying that constitutes plagiarism under your policy.
  • Bias amplification and privacy leakage — company data enters public tools.

Ten guardrails for AI-assisted DBA research

Treat GenAI as a research instrument to be validated — like a survey you pilot-test — never as a co-author. These principles translate institutional policy into daily practice.

01

Disclose. Declare AI use in your methodology and per your institution's policy — disclosure is the floor, not the ceiling.

02

Verify. Never cite a reference you have not opened yourself — GenAI hallucinates citations.

03

Own the analysis. The final interpretation, decisions, and claims are yours — AI assists, it does not decide.

04

Keep the human in the loop. Review every AI output for accuracy, bias, and fit before it touches the dissertation.

05

Protect data. No confidential, proprietary, or personal data in public tools — anonymise, consent, and use approved environments.

06

Keep ethics ahead. Ethics approval covers AI-assisted handling of human data too — write it into your protocol.

07

Keep an audit trail. Log prompts and outputs so the process is transparent and reproducible.

08

Cite the tool. If AI-generated content enters the text, cite it according to your citation guide.

09

Interrogate bias. Probe outputs for systematic error — an "authoritative" AI statement is a hypothesis, not a finding.

10

Match tool to task. GenAI for drafting and discovery; statistical software for analysis; you for judgement.

Your AI course, your advantage. Because you are studying AI and Generative AI, you can critique the instrument while using it — that is the difference between a DBA who is informed by AI and one who is replaced by it. Before you begin, read your institution's AI and academic-integrity policy, confirm your supervisor's and programme's stance, and agree in writing what may be automated.

12 · The players

Stakeholders, accountability, and the research constitution

A DBA is not solo work. Every thesis is produced inside a web of players — each with a distinct attitude, set of responsibilities, line of accountability, ethics and values, and a formal constitution — the documents that bind them. Knowing who answers for what prevents the failures that sink dissertations: ethics slips, supervision drift, and murky ownership.

Honesty Integrity Transparency Respect Beneficence Justice Confidentiality Rigour Fairness Reflexivity
01 You

The researcher

Attitude — critical, curious, humble, reflexive; owns the work and the final judgement.

Responsibilities — design, conduct, analyse, and write; disclose AI use; protect data; meet milestones.

Accountability — to supervisor and program, and through them to examiners and the public.

Ethics & values — honesty, integrity, transparency, respect for participants, no fabrication.

Constitution — program handbook, academic-integrity and AI policies, supervisor agreement, ethics approval.

02 Mentor

Supervisor / guide

Attitude — challenging but supportive; the quality gatekeeper of the candidature.

Responsibilities — advise on scope, method, and standards; review drafts; flag integrity issues; manage milestones.

Accountability — to the program and university for the quality and integrity of supervision.

Ethics & values — availability, constructive critique, intellectual honesty, no ghost-writing.

Constitution — supervision guidelines, faculty code, program regulations, supervision agreement.

03 Institution

Program & university

Attitude — the institutional guardian of standards and of the degree's credibility.

Responsibilities — set regulations, outcomes, and milestones; assure quality; award the degree; provide resources.

Accountability — to accreditors and the public for the credibility of the award.

Ethics & values — fairness, consistency, accountability, public trust.

Constitution — academic regulations, degree specifications, research degree framework, accreditation standards.

04 Gatekeeper

Ethics committee / IRB

Attitude — risk-aware guardian of participants, not an obstacle to be passed.

Responsibilities — review and approve research involving humans or data; monitor compliance; advise on data protection.

Accountability — to the university and, where relevant, to regulatory bodies.

Ethics & values — beneficence, respect for persons, justice; consent; confidentiality.

Constitution — ethics approval process, data-protection law, Belmont principles, disciplinary codes.

05 Independent

Examiners / viva panel

Attitude — rigorous, independent, and fair critics of the finished thesis.

Responsibilities — assess against standards; conduct the viva; recommend pass, corrections, or revision.

Accountability — to the university for defensible, independent judgement.

Ethics & values — impartiality, confidentiality, fairness, rigour.

Constitution — examination regulations, assessment criteria, external examiner rules.

06 Voluntary

Participants

Attitude — voluntary contributors whose time and data are lent, never owned.

Responsibilities — provide information freely; the researcher owes them care in return.

Accountability — the researcher is accountable to them and the ethics committee for their safety and privacy.

Ethics & values — informed consent, voluntariness, anonymity, right to withdraw.

Constitution — consent forms, participant information sheets, data-protection rights.

07 Partner

Case organisation / sponsor

Attitude — interested partner granting access; expects relevance and confidentiality in return.

Responsibilities — grant data access and credibility; the researcher returns practical value and findings.

Accountability — researcher is accountable for confidentiality; the organisation cannot steer findings.

Ethics & values — confidentiality, reciprocity, independence of findings, no undue influence.

Constitution — access letters, non-disclosure and data-sharing agreements, IP agreements.

08 Instrument

13 · Research writing

Writing research so a reader can follow the argument

A good study can be lost in bad writing. Academic writing is a craft with two tests: can the reader follow the argument without re-reading, and can every claim be checked against the evidence? The moves below — clarity, precision, evidence-led claims, calibrated certainty — are what separate a defensible thesis from a report.

Good writing is…

  • Clear on first read. One idea per sentence; the reader is never asked to re-read.
  • Precise. Named actors, real numbers, and bounded scope instead of "many" and "some".
  • Dense. More meaning per word; no throat-clearing.
  • Evidence-led. Every claim carries a source or a datum.
  • Coherent. Each sentence grows from the last; transitions signpost the route.
  • Honest about certainty. "Suggests" where it suggests, "shows" where it shows.

Bad writing is…

  • Opaque. Nominalisations ("the utilisation of") and passive piles ("it was decided").
  • Vague. "A significant impact" with no size, direction, or actor.
  • Inflated. "In today's fast-paced business environment" says nothing.
  • Unsupported. Confident assertions with no citation or data.
  • Drifting. Paragraphs that wander; the point arrives last, if at all.
  • Overclaimed. "Proves", "always", "clearly" where the evidence only suggests.

Weak vs strong, dimension by dimension

Use this as a first-pass diagnostic of your own draft. If most of your sentences land in the left column, revise for the right.

Dimension Weak Strong

The same idea, written weak and strong

Both paragraphs argue the same thing — that lean efficiency is the mechanism of fragility under demand shocks. One reads like a first draft; the other is the thesis version. The annotation list below says what changed and why.

Weak — how first drafts usually read

Due to the fact that the supply chain system of Walmart has been very successful in the past, it is important for researchers to consider the various factors that might be relevant when thinking about the potential for disruptions in the future. There are a lot of different things that can go wrong with supply chains. One of the biggest problems is when there is a disruption and the company is not able to fulfill the orders of its customers. In recent times, the COVID-19 pandemic was a very good example of this happening, and it was a very bad time for a lot of companies.

Strong — rewritten with the moves

Lean just-in-time inventory is the mechanism of supply chain fragility, not its antidote: with no buffer stock, a demand shock travels straight to the shelf. Pandemic-era stockouts show the cost. Retail forecasting models are trained on stable, historical demand and have not been validated against panic-buying behaviour (Holweg, 2007; Ivanov, 2020); when behaviour departs from history, service levels collapse. The exposure is not Walmart-specific — every demand-driven retailer faces the same vulnerability. This study therefore asks how forecast models perform under exogenous demand shocks, and which adjustments preserve service levels.

What changed — and why

  • Cut the throat-clearing. "Due to the fact that…" and "it is important for researchers to consider" vanish; the first sentence now states the claim.
  • Named the actor. "The supply chain system of Walmart" becomes "lean just-in-time inventory" — the construct the whole thesis studies.
  • One idea per sentence. The weak version bundles five ideas into two sentences; the strong version gives each idea its own sentence.
  • Evidence carried the claim. Citations (Holweg, 2007; Ivanov, 2020) replaced "a very good example".
  • Calibrated certainty. "Very bad", "very big", and "always" became "show the cost" and "every demand-driven retailer faces the same vulnerability".
  • Ended with the question. The last sentence hands the reader the research question — the paragraph's whole job.

The ten moves, as a toolkit

Run every paragraph you write through these. If a paragraph fails one move, it fails the reader.

01Clarity

Clear before clever

Short sentences, plain words, and the reader should never have to re-read. Cleverness is a reward for clarity, not a substitute for it.

02Precision

Name actors, numbers, scope

"Large retailers" → "the 100 largest US retailers by revenue". Vague quantifiers ("many", "some") are placeholders for missing evidence.

03Density

More meaning per word

Cut "it is important to note that", "there is / there are", and double phrases ("attempt to try"). If a word adds no meaning, delete it.

04Coherence

Each sentence grows from the last

Put the connection where the reader looks: the new sentence opens with what the previous one established. Transitions are signposts, not decorations.

05Evidence-led

No citation, no claim

Every assertion carries a source or a datum traceable to the evidence ledger. Uncited confidence is the fastest way to lose an examiner.

06Voice

Active, responsible verbs

"We analysed", "the model predicts" — the writer owns the analysis. Reserve the passive for when the actor is genuinely unknown.

07Hedging

Calibrate certainty

"Suggests" vs "shows" vs "under X conditions" is scholarly credibility. Overclaiming is the most common examiner complaint — and the most avoidable.

08Structure

Signpost the plan

State the point first, then support it. Tell the reader where the section is going and what it concluded — twice, once at each end.

09Plain words

Jargon only where it earns its place

Keep discipline terms (they carry theory); cut bureaucratic filler — "utilise" → "use", "endeavour" → "try". Definitions belong in the thesis, not the sentence.

10Critical

Argue, don't summarise

The literature review is a debate, not a bibliography. Every source is cited to do a job: support, contrast, or be corrected.

Common faults, and the fix

Every one of these appeared in real doctoral drafts. Learn to spot them in your own.

Fault Weak example Fix
Throat-clearing"In today's rapidly evolving business environment…"Delete. Say what actually changed.
Redundancy"It is important to note that lean systems reduce inventory.""Lean systems reduce inventory."
Nominalisation"The utilisation of the just-in-time methodology was undertaken.""Walmart uses just-in-time (JIT) methods."
Vague quantifier"There are many factors that influence resilience."Name the factors and their direction.
Unsupported scale"Walmart is a very big and successful company.""Walmart operates about 10,500 stores across 20+ countries" — with a source.
Passive pile-up"It was decided that the data would be analysed.""We analysed the data."
Hedge stacking"It could possibly be argued that lean may perhaps…"One hedge, chosen: "The evidence suggests that lean…"
Overclaim"This proves that JIT causes fragility.""Under stable demand JIT outperforms; under shocks it is exposed."

Checklists to run your draft through

Sentence level

  • One idea per sentence.
  • Subject sits next to the verb.
  • Active voice by default.
  • Strong verbs, specific nouns.
  • No "there is / there are" openings.
  • Every hedge is intentional.

Paragraph level

  • Opens with the claim (topic sentence).
  • Evidence follows the claim.
  • Warrant connects evidence to claim.
  • Ends with a link to the next point.
  • One job per paragraph.

Thesis level

  • Point-first structure throughout.
  • Sections signposted; each answers "so what?".
  • Every claim traceable to the evidence ledger.
  • Terminology consistent from chapter to chapter.
  • Citation discipline: every source verified and cited.
Writing is thinking made visible. If a paragraph is hard to write, the argument is still half-formed — clarify the argument first, and the sentence obeys. Budget revision time in three passes: structure, sentences, then proof. And run the ten moves on the abstract last; the abstract is the whole thesis in miniature.
